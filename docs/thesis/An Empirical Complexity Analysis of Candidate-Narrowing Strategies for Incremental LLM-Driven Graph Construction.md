# Chapter 1: The Problem and Its Background

## 1.1 Background of the Study

A graph is one of the most fundamental structures in computer science: a collection of *nodes* (also called vertices) connected by *edges* (also called links or relationships). Graphs are used to represent anything that consists of individual things and the connections between them — a road network is a graph where cities are nodes and roads are edges; a social network is a graph where people are nodes and friendships are edges. This simplicity is what makes graphs powerful: many real-world problems can be reframed as questions about a graph's structure, such as finding the shortest path between two nodes, or determining how tightly connected a group of nodes is.

A **knowledge graph** is a specific application of this idea, where nodes represent *concepts*, *entities*, or *topics*, and edges represent *meaningful relationships* between them — for example, "Photosynthesis" and "Cellular Respiration" might be connected by an edge labeled "is related to," or "Calculus" and "Limits" might be connected by an edge labeled "requires." Unlike a simple list or a table, a knowledge graph can capture how ideas relate to one another in ways that support *navigation* — a person or a system can start at one concept and traverse the graph to discover related concepts, prerequisite concepts, or dependent concepts. Knowledge graphs are used across many domains, including web search, recommendation systems, and, increasingly, education — where they can represent how topics in a subject build on one another.

Historically, building a knowledge graph required significant manual effort: domain experts had to read source material, identify the relevant concepts, and manually specify the relationships between them. This process, while accurate, does not scale — a graph covering a large domain, or a graph that must be kept up to date as new information becomes available, would require an enormous and continuous amount of expert labor.

The emergence of **Large Language Models (LLMs)** — AI systems trained on vast amounts of text, capable of understanding and generating natural language — has changed this. LLMs can be prompted to read unstructured text and extract candidate concepts and relationships automatically, a task that previously required a human expert. This has enabled a wave of automated knowledge graph construction systems, where the overall process typically follows three stages: first, defining what *kinds* of concepts and relationships are relevant to the domain (*ontology engineering*); second, extracting candidate concepts and relationships from source text (*knowledge extraction*); and third, merging everything into one coherent, non-redundant graph (*knowledge fusion*).

However, most existing systems treat this as a **one-time, batch process**: an LLM is given an entire corpus of source material and asked to produce a complete graph in a single pass. This works well when the goal is to build a graph once, but it does not reflect how knowledge actually grows. In practice, new concepts continue to emerge after a graph has already been built — a course adds a new topic, a research field publishes a new technique, an encyclopedia adds a new article. Repeating the full batch-construction process every time a single new concept needs to be added is wasteful: it means re-examining a graph that may already contain thousands of existing nodes, just to correctly place one new one.

This is the **incremental insertion problem**: given an existing knowledge graph and one new candidate concept, how should that concept be connected to the *existing* graph, without re-examining every node that already exists? A naive approach — asking an LLM to compare the new concept against every single existing node, one at a time — is described directly in the literature as impractical, since it requires "a huge number of queries to the LLM" as the graph grows \[1\]. Funk et al. \[1\] report that even for modest domains, their brute-force approach required an average of 22 to 50 LLM prompts per newly inserted concept, at a real monetary cost ranging from \$0.11 to \$12.45 depending on the domain — a cost that would grow substantially for larger, real-world graphs.

To avoid this brute-force cost, more recent systems adopt a **two-stage narrowing strategy**: first, use a cheap, fast method — typically comparing vector *embeddings* (numerical representations of a concept's meaning, where similar concepts have mathematically similar vectors) — to quickly narrow down a small set of *candidate* existing nodes that the new concept might relate to; then, use the more expensive but more accurate LLM only to decide *among those candidates*, rather than against the entire graph. iText2KG \[2\], for instance, uses a cosine similarity threshold of approximately 0.6 to filter candidate matches before invoking an LLM. More recent systems have proposed alternative narrowing mechanisms: SAC-KG \[3\] uses a trained classifier to decide, per candidate node, whether the graph should grow or stop expanding in that direction, validated at a scale of over one million nodes with 89.32% precision; and EraRAG \[4\] organizes nodes into size-bounded "buckets," such that inserting a new node only ever requires modifying one bounded bucket rather than searching the whole graph, a design for which the authors provide a formal proof (their Theorem 4\) claiming an amortized insertion cost of O(Δ(nd \+ S\_LLM)) — meaning, informally, that the cost of an insertion should not grow simply because the existing graph has grown.

This body of work establishes that the *idea* of narrowing candidates before invoking an LLM is well-established and actively researched. However, a closer reading of this literature reveals two consistent, related gaps that motivate the present study.

First, **no reviewed study empirically measures insertion cost as an explicit function of the existing[^1] graph's size.** Systems report aggregate cost figures — total dollars spent, total tokens consumed, or total time taken across an entire experiment — but do not report how the cost of *inserting one new node* changes as the graph grows from, for example, 50 nodes to 2,000 nodes. EraRAG's \[4\] own dynamic experiments illustrate this precisely: every reported insertion experiment holds the pre-existing corpus at a roughly fixed size (splitting a dataset into a 50% "initial" portion and inserting the remaining 50% in fixed increments) and varies only the *size of the incoming batch*, never the *size of the graph being inserted into*. This means that even a system with a formally proven complexity bound has not, in its own published results, empirically demonstrated that the bound holds as the underlying graph itself scales up — the theoretical claim and the empirical demonstration address two different variables.

Second, and more specifically, **no reviewed study compares multiple candidate-narrowing strategies against one another, under the same task and the same measurements.** Existing systems each propose and evaluate their *own* narrowing mechanism against generic system-level baselines, but do not isolate and compare the narrowing mechanism itself — for instance, is a bounded-bucket structure \[4\] actually faster than a straightforward embedding-similarity threshold \[2\], at the same graph size, on the same insertion task? Without this comparison, it is not possible to say which narrowing strategy is preferable, or under what conditions one might outperform another.

It is worth noting explicitly that these two gaps were identified through a structured but non-exhaustive literature search conducted as part of this study's preliminary investigation, combining general web search, targeted citation-trail searches, and direct reading of primary sources \[1\]–\[4\], \[6\].

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

This study addresses the general problem that, within the growing body of research on LLM-driven incremental knowledge graph construction \[1\]–\[5\], the practical cost and comparative behavior of different node-insertion strategies remain unmeasured as a graph scales in size.

Specifically, this study seeks to answer the following questions:

1. How does the computational cost of inserting a new node into an existing LLM-constructed knowledge graph — measured in wall-clock time and number of LLM API calls — change as the size of the existing graph increases?  
2. How does the placement *accuracy* of an inserted node — measured against an expert-verified gold-standard graph structure \[6\], \[7\] — change as the size of the existing graph increases?  
3. How do multiple candidate-narrowing strategies (specifically: brute-force comparison \[1\], embedding-similarity thresholding \[2\], approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, and bounded-bucket partitioning \[4\]) compare to one another on the above two measures, under identical experimental conditions?  
4. To what extent do the empirical cost-scaling behaviors observed in this study match the theoretical complexity claims made in existing literature — specifically, the amortized bound proposed by \[4\] for bucket-based insertion?

## 1.3 Objectives of the Study

**General Objective:** To empirically characterize and compare the cost and accuracy of candidate-narrowing strategies for inserting new nodes into an existing, LLM-constructed knowledge graph, as the size of that graph increases.

**Specific Objectives:**

1. To implement four candidate-narrowing strategies for knowledge graph node insertion, drawing on approaches described in \[1\], \[2\], and \[4\]: (a) brute-force LLM comparison, (b) embedding-similarity thresholding, (c) approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) retrieval, and (d) bounded-bucket partitioning.  
2. To design and execute an experiment that inserts new nodes into knowledge graphs of increasing size (following a log-spaced progression, e.g., n \= 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000 nodes) using each of the four strategies.  
3. To measure, for each strategy at each graph size, the wall-clock time per insertion, the number of LLM API calls per insertion, and the placement accuracy of the inserted node relative to an expert-verified gold-standard structure \[6\], \[7\].  
4. To analyze whether the observed cost-scaling behavior is consistent with the theoretical complexity claims found in \[4\].  
5. To produce a documented, reproducible experimental artifact (codebase and dataset) that can be extended by future researchers studying incremental knowledge graph construction.

## 1.4 Significance of the Study

**To the field of Computer Science / Knowledge Graph Research.** This study contributes an empirical benchmark that does not currently exist in the reviewed literature \[1\]–\[4\]: a controlled, side-by-side comparison of multiple node-insertion strategies as a function of graph size. This directly addresses a gap identified in current published systems, where insertion cost is reported in aggregate but not as a scaling function, and where no single study compares more than one narrowing strategy under identical conditions.

**To Future Researchers.** The findings of this study — particularly whether theoretical complexity claims such as those in \[4\] hold up empirically — provide a foundation that future work can build on, whether by extending the comparison to additional strategies, applying the methodology to new domains, or investigating the specific points at which a given strategy's performance degrades.

**To Practitioners Building Knowledge-Graph Systems.** Developers building systems that require ongoing knowledge graph maintenance may use this study's findings to inform which narrowing strategy is most appropriate for their expected graph size and update frequency.

## 1.5 Scope and Limitations

This study is limited to the **incremental insertion phase** of knowledge graph construction — the process of adding one new node to an *already-existing* graph. It does not address the initial batch-construction process in depth beyond what is necessary to produce a starting graph for the insertion experiments, nor does it propose a novel batch-construction method as a contribution.

This study evaluates candidate-narrowing strategies using two complementary data sources: (1) publicly available, expert-verified gold-standard educational knowledge graphs released by Aytekin and Saygın \[6\], \[7\] — specifically the Metacademy graph (141 concepts from Machine Learning, Statistics, and Linear Algebra) and the DSA graph (29 concepts from Data Structures and Algorithms) — used to measure placement *accuracy* at smaller graph sizes where expert ground truth is available; and (2) synthetically generated or unlabeled concept sets, used to extend the *cost-scaling* measurements to larger graph sizes (up to approximately 2,000 nodes) where expert-verified ground truth is not required, since cost metrics can be measured objectively without a gold-standard structure.

This study does not evaluate the narrowing strategies within an educational application context; the contribution is restricted to the underlying algorithmic mechanism, independent of any specific downstream use case. This is a deliberate scope decision: the educational domain in \[6\], \[7\] is used only as a source of gold-standard evaluation data, not as the study's motivating application.

This study is limited to a fixed, publicly available set of candidate-narrowing strategies, drawing on the general approaches described in \[1\], \[2\], and \[4\], and does not propose a novel narrowing algorithm as its primary contribution; rather, its contribution is the empirical comparison itself.

## 1.6 Definition of Terms

- **Node (Vertex):** A single unit of data in a graph, representing a concept, entity, or topic.  
- **Edge:** A connection between two nodes, representing a relationship.  
- **Knowledge Graph (KG):** A graph structure in which nodes represent concepts and edges represent meaningful semantic relationships between them.  
- **Large Language Model (LLM):** An artificial intelligence model trained on large volumes of text, capable of understanding and generating natural language, and capable of being prompted to perform reasoning-like tasks such as determining whether a relationship exists between two given concepts.  
- **Embedding:** A numerical (vector) representation of a piece of text, designed such that texts with similar meaning have mathematically similar vector representations.  
- **Cosine Similarity:** A mathematical measure of how similar two vectors are, based on the angle between them, commonly used to compare embeddings \[2\].  
- **Incremental Insertion:** The process of adding a single new node to an already-existing graph, as opposed to constructing a graph from scratch (batch construction).  
- **Candidate-Narrowing Strategy:** A method used to reduce the number of existing nodes that must be considered when deciding how to connect a newly inserted node, in order to avoid the cost described in \[1\].  
- **Approximate Nearest-Neighbor (ANN) Retrieval:** A technique for efficiently finding the items in a large collection that are most similar to a given query item, without necessarily checking every item exactly.  
- **Amortized Complexity:** A way of analyzing an algorithm's cost by averaging it over a sequence of operations, rather than analyzing the worst-case cost of a single operation in isolation — the analytical basis for the complexity claim in \[4\].  
- **Gold-Standard Graph:** A knowledge graph whose structure has been verified by domain experts, used as a ground-truth reference \[6\], \[7\].

## 1.7 Theoretical / Conceptual Framework

This study is grounded in three theoretical foundations:

**1\. Graph Theory**, which provides the formal mathematical basis for representing concepts and relationships as nodes and edges, and for reasoning about properties such as connectivity, traversal, and structural growth over time.

**2\. Amortized Algorithm Analysis**, a foundational technique from classical computer science which provides the analytical framework for reasoning about the cost of a sequence of insertions, rather than a single insertion in isolation. This study's central empirical question — whether real-world insertion cost matches theoretical amortized bounds such as the one proposed in \[4\] — is a direct application of this framework to a new setting.

**3\. Vector Semantics and Embedding-Based Similarity**, which provides the theoretical basis for the "narrowing" step common to modern insertion strategies \[2\], \[3\]: the idea that semantic relatedness between concepts can be approximated by geometric proximity between their vector representations, enabling fast candidate filtering prior to more expensive LLM-based reasoning.

**Conceptual Framework (Input → Process → Output):**

- **Input:** An existing knowledge graph of size *n*, a new candidate concept to be inserted, and a chosen candidate-narrowing strategy.  
- **Process:** The candidate-narrowing strategy filters the existing graph down to a small set of plausible connection points; an LLM then determines the precise relationship (if any) between the new concept and each candidate; the new node and its determined edges are added to the graph; cost and accuracy are measured.  
- **Output:** An updated knowledge graph of size *n+1*, along with measurements of insertion cost and placement accuracy, compared across strategies and across increasing values of *n*.

# Chapter 2: Review of Related Literature

## 2.1 Foreign Literature

### 2.1.1 Knowledge Graphs and Their Construction

The theoretical foundation for representing structured knowledge as a graph of interconnected concepts predates the current generation of AI systems by decades, but the *automated construction* of such graphs using AI is a comparatively recent development. Prior to the widespread availability of large language models, knowledge graph construction relied heavily on either fully manual curation by domain experts or on supervised machine learning pipelines trained on hand-labeled data — both approaches requiring substantial upfront human effort that limited how quickly a graph could be built or updated.

The general pattern across LLM-era construction systems — extracting candidate entities and relations from text, then merging them into a coherent structure — reflects the classical three-stage view of knowledge graph construction: ontology engineering, knowledge extraction, and knowledge fusion. What LLMs have changed is not this overall structure, but the *cost and speed* of the extraction stage specifically, since a general-purpose LLM can be prompted to perform extraction without the domain-specific rule-writing or model training that earlier automated approaches required.

### 2.1.2 Amortized Complexity Analysis

Separately, and originating in an entirely different research tradition, the field of algorithm analysis has long studied how to reason about the cost of operations that modify a data structure repeatedly over time — insertions into a dynamic array, insertions into a self-balancing tree, or updates to a spatial data structure such as a Voronoi diagram. The relevant analytical tool here is *amortized complexity*: rather than analyzing the worst-case cost of a single operation, amortized analysis considers the average cost across a sequence of operations, which often reveals that an occasional expensive operation (such as a data structure resizing itself) is "paid for" by many preceding cheap operations, yielding a favorable average cost overall.

This body of classical analysis is directly relevant to the present study because it supplies the precise vocabulary and proof techniques — accounting arguments, potential-function arguments, and geometric-decay arguments — that would be needed to rigorously establish whether a knowledge-graph insertion strategy's cost is truly bounded as the graph grows, rather than merely observed to be low in a specific experiment.

## 2.2 Foreign Studies

### 2.2.1 The Brute-Force Insertion Problem

Funk, Hosemann, Jung, and Lutz \[1\] were among the first to explicitly formalize the cost problem this study investigates. In their work on constructing ontological hierarchies using LLMs, they identify that naively querying an LLM to compare a new concept against every existing concept in a hierarchy — in order to determine subsumption relationships — becomes impractical as the hierarchy grows, since it requires, in their words, "a huge number of queries to the LLM" \[1\]. To address this, they propose an insertion algorithm adapted from the classical KRIS algorithm (originally developed for description-logic subsumption testing), which searches the existing hierarchy top-down and bottom-up, pruning branches once a subsumption test fails, rather than testing every possible pair. Their reported results — averaging 22 to 50 LLM prompts per inserted concept, at costs ranging from \$0.11 to \$12.45 across nine tested domains — provide concrete, quantified evidence of the cost this study's proposed narrowing strategies aim to reduce. Notably, Funk et al. \[1\] also report a methodological limitation directly relevant to this study: LLM-derived subsumption judgments were found to be non-transitive in practice — that is, the LLM could affirm that A is a subclass of B, and that B is a subclass of C, while denying that A is a subclass of C, despite transitivity being a logical requirement of the subsumption relation being modeled. The authors address this by simply assuming transitivity holds, rather than verifying it — a methodological choice that this study takes note of as a known open issue in LLM-based hierarchy construction, though it falls outside this study's own scope.

### 2.2.2 Embedding-Based Candidate Narrowing

Lairgi, Moncla, Cazabet, Benabdeslem, and Cléau \[2\] propose iText2KG, a system explicitly designed for *incremental* knowledge graph construction — directly addressing the batch-only limitation of earlier systems. Central to their approach is an "iEntities Matcher" module, which computes cosine similarity between a new candidate entity's embedding and existing entities in the graph, using an empirically-derived similarity threshold (approximately 0.6, with a standard deviation of 0.12, calibrated against a dataset of 1,500 GPT-4-generated similar-entity pairs) to decide whether the new entity matches an existing one before invoking further processing. Their reported false discovery rate for unresolved entities (0.01 on scientific articles, 0 on CVs) substantially outperforms comparison systems such as LangChain and LlamaIndex-based pipelines (which the authors report at 0.11–0.31 in the same evaluation), demonstrating that embedding-based narrowing can be both fast and accurate for the specific matching task it targets.

Two details of this study are worth emphasizing for the present work. First, the authors explicitly identify, as a direction for future work, "eliminating the necessity to define a threshold as a hyperparameter" \[2\] — an open problem this study does not attempt to solve, but which motivates comparing threshold-based narrowing against alternative strategies that do not rely on a single fixed hyperparameter (such as approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, which retrieves a fixed *number* of candidates rather than filtering by a fixed similarity *value*). Second, it is worth noting, based on the project's own public version history, that the iText2KG system was later modified to rely on pure cosine-similarity comparison for entity resolution rather than combining it with LLM-based decision-making — a change the maintainers describe as being for "scalable, parallel merging." This detail suggests that even the original authors of an LLM-assisted narrowing strategy found reason to reduce their system's reliance on LLM invocation as a scalability measure, a consideration directly relevant to this study's comparison of narrowing strategies by cost.

### 2.2.3 Learned Pruning for Large-Scale Construction

Chen, Shen, Lv, Wang, Ni, and Ye \[3\] propose SAC-KG, a system built around three cooperating components: a Generator, which produces candidate relations and entities for a given node; a Verifier, which checks generated content for correctness; and a Pruner, which decides whether a given branch of the graph should continue expanding or stop. The Pruner component is of particular relevance to this study, as it represents a concrete, published mechanism for automating the "should this node be added" decision at scale — the authors report successful construction of a domain knowledge graph exceeding one million nodes, at 89.32% precision, representing an improvement of over 20 percentage points compared to prior state-of-the-art construction methods. This result establishes that automated, LLM-informed pruning decisions are viable at a scale far beyond what this study's own experiments will attempt, though SAC-KG's evaluation, like the studies above, does not report how pruning decision cost scales as a function of the *existing* graph's size specifically. ### 2.2.4 Bounded-Structure Incremental Updates and Their Theoretical Claims

Zhang, Huang, Zhou, Guo, Li, Luo, Jiang, Fang, and Zhou \[4\] propose EraRAG, a hierarchical graph-based retrieval system designed for growing text corpora. Structurally, EraRAG organizes incoming data into size-bounded "buckets" using a hyperplane-based locality-sensitive hashing scheme; because bucket size is capped by a small constant, inserting new data only ever requires modifying a bounded, constant-sized region of the graph, regardless of how large the graph has already grown. The authors formally prove this property as their Theorem 4, establishing an amortized time complexity of O(Δ(nd \+ S\_LLM)) for inserting Δ new items — a bound that, if it holds, implies insertion cost should not increase merely because the pre-existing graph has grown larger.

This claim is of direct and central relevance to the present study for two reasons. First, it is, among all works reviewed, the only one to offer a formal proof of an amortized cost bound specifically for LLM-graph insertion, making it the strongest available theoretical anchor for this study's central research question. Second, and more importantly for motivating this study's contribution, a close reading of EraRAG's own reported experiments reveals that this bound is never empirically validated against the specific variable it claims to be independent of: total pre-existing graph size. Every dynamic-insertion experiment reported by the authors holds the pre-existing corpus at a fixed proportion (typically splitting a dataset into a 50% "initial" portion, with the remaining 50% inserted in fixed increments) and varies only the size of the *incoming batch*, never the size of the *graph being inserted into*. The proof itself — while broadly consistent with established amortized-analysis techniques such as those described in Section 2.1.2 — warrants closer scrutiny: the proof's central step — asserting that repeated bucket splits and merges will not cascade without formally invoking a standard amortized-analysis technique such as a potential-function or accounting argument — falls short of the rigor typically expected of such a bound, a gap this study's proposed empirical measurement is directly designed to address.

### 2.2.5 Expert-Verified Prerequisite Graph Construction

Aytekin and Saygın \[6\] present ACE, a system for constructing Educational Knowledge Graphs (EKGs) — knowledge graphs specifically representing *prerequisite* relationships among concepts in a learning domain, intended to support student navigation through a subject's dependency structure. Unlike the general-purpose systems discussed above, ACE combines an embedding-based prerequisite-scoring mechanism with a human-expert-in-the-loop verification step: concept pairs are ranked by a computed similarity score, and only the highest-ranked pairs are presented to a human expert for final confirmation, substantially reducing the total number of pairs requiring manual review compared to full manual construction.

Two aspects of this work are especially significant to the present study. First, Aytekin and Saygın \[6\] directly compare their embedding-based scoring approach against zero-shot and retrieval-augmented LLM baselines (GPT-4o-mini, LLaMA 3\) on the prerequisite-detection task itself, reporting a clear precision-recall trade-off: their embedding-based approach achieves near-complete recall (100%) but modest precision (46.13%), while LLM-based approaches achieve much higher precision (up to 87.23% with retrieval augmentation) but substantially lower recall (as low as 35.80% in the zero-shot case) — demonstrating that neither embeddings nor LLMs alone are sufficient for reliably identifying prerequisite relationships, a trade-off this study's own comparison of narrowing strategies must be interpreted in light of. Second, and most directly relevant to this study's methodology, Aytekin and Saygın \[6\] publicly released the gold-standard graphs used in their own evaluation — including the Metacademy graph (141 expert-verified concepts from Machine Learning, Statistics, and Linear Algebra) and the DSA graph (29 expert-verified concepts from Data Structures and Algorithms) \[7\] — which this study adopts directly as its source of expert-verified ground truth for measuring insertion placement accuracy, as detailed in Chapter 1's Scope and Limitations and to be further specified in Chapter 3\.

## 2.3 Local Literature and Studies

A structured search of Philippine-affiliated academic output — including general web search, targeted searches of Philippine computing-research venues such as the *International Journal of Computing Sciences Research*, and searches specifically combining Philippine-institutional terms with knowledge-graph and large-language-model terminology — found no Philippine-affiliated study addressing LLM-driven knowledge graph construction, incremental graph insertion, or candidate-narrowing strategies for graph maintenance. This absence is treated here as a finding to be reported honestly rather than a search failure to be concealed: it is corroborated by an independent, unrelated finding reported by Balilo \[9\], whose review of Bicol University's CS/IT capstone submissions from 2011 to 2019 found that approximately 60% of Philippine undergraduate capstone projects in computing take the form of information systems built for a specific organization, suggesting that a purely algorithmic, mechanism-level study such as the present one — with no organizational client or information-system deliverable — represents a genuine minority pattern within the local capstone research tradition, rather than an oversight specific to the knowledge-graph subfield.

One Philippine-affiliated study, however, is closely related in subject matter and is discussed here in detail. Naguio and Roxas \[10\] present a multi-scale graph neural network framework for *knowledge graph completion* — the task of predicting missing relationships within an existing graph — applied specifically to Philippine lexical resources spanning Tagalog, Cebuano, and English, evaluated on a graph of 113,557 words and 304,171 lexical relations. Their approach processes graph neighborhoods at three distinct scales (local morphological patterns, semantic clusters, and cross-lingual relationships) and reports a substantial improvement over baseline methods (76.01% AUC, a 22.90 percentage-point gain).

While methodologically distinct from the present study — Naguio and Roxas address graph neural network-based completion over a fixed, pre-existing node set, rather than LLM-driven placement of newly arriving nodes — this work is conceptually adjacent in an important respect: both studies are concerned with modifying an *already-existing* graph structure efficiently, rather than constructing a graph from scratch. Their multi-scale neighborhood approach (processing candidates at multiple structural distances simultaneously) is also a design pattern worth noting alongside this study's own candidate-narrowing strategies, even though the underlying mechanism (graph neural network message-passing) differs substantially from the embedding-and-LLM-based approaches this study investigates. This study's contribution differs from theirs primarily in its object of measurement: where Naguio and Roxas measure completion accuracy, this study measures insertion *cost* as a function of graph size, a dimension not addressed in their work.

## 2.4 Synthesis

Table 2.1 summarizes the works reviewed in this chapter according to three dimensions directly relevant to this study's contribution: whether the work addresses incremental (rather than purely batch) graph construction, whether it proposes or evaluates a specific candidate-narrowing mechanism, and whether it empirically measures cost as an explicit function of pre-existing graph size.

Across the seven works discussed in Sections 2.2 and 2.3, every study that addresses incremental knowledge graph construction proposes and evaluates its own narrowing mechanism in isolation, but none evaluates that mechanism *against* an alternative narrowing mechanism under the same task and measurement conditions \[1\]–\[4\]. Separately, every study that reports a cost or efficiency measurement does so in aggregate — total tokens, total dollars, or total time across an entire experimental run — rather than as an explicit function of the size of the graph being inserted into at the time of insertion \[1\]–\[4\]. This holds even for the one study offering a formal theoretical bound on insertion cost \[4\], whose own empirical validation, as discussed in Section 2.2.4, varies a different variable (incoming batch size) than the one its proof concerns (pre-existing graph size).

This chapter's review therefore surfaces two specific, well-evidenced gaps that directly motivate the present study, first identified in Chapter 1 and reinforced here through closer engagement with the primary literature: (1) the absence of any reviewed study empirically measuring insertion cost as a function of pre-existing graph size, and (2) the absence of any reviewed study comparing multiple candidate-narrowing strategies against one another under controlled, identical conditions. It should be stated plainly that this is an absence claim, and absence claims are inherently limited by the scope of the search that produced them; the search conducted for this chapter, while combining multiple search strategies and direct engagement with primary sources, was not an exhaustive systematic review of the kind that would typically require a dedicated methodology of its own (e.g., a PRISMA-style protocol across multiple academic databases). This limitation is noted here in the interest of methodological honesty and is not expected to materially change this study's motivation, given the consistency of the absence across every relevant work independently identified through several distinct search strategies.

# Chapter 3: Methodology

## 3.1 Research Design

This study employs a **quantitative, experimental research design**. It does not involve human respondents, surveys, or qualitative interpretation of subjective data; instead, it involves the controlled manipulation of an independent variable (the candidate-narrowing strategy used, and the size of the graph being inserted into) and the measurement of dependent variables (insertion cost and insertion accuracy) under repeatable, controlled conditions. This makes the study's design closer to a **algorithmic benchmark** than to a survey-based or descriptive study, and its methodology is structured accordingly around implementation, experimental procedure, and measurement — rather than around instrument validity, sampling, or respondent demographics.

## 3.2 System Architecture and Implementation

### 3.2.1 Overview of the Experimental Pipeline

The experimental system consists of four independently implemented **candidate-narrowing strategies**, each of which performs the same fundamental task — given an existing knowledge graph and one new candidate concept, decide how (or whether) that concept connects to the existing graph — using a different underlying mechanism. All four strategies share the same final decision-making step (an LLM call that determines the precise relationship between the new concept and a shortlisted set of candidates); they differ only in *how that shortlist is produced*, which is the specific variable this study investigates. ### 3.2.2 Strategy A: Brute-Force Comparison

This strategy serves as the study's control condition. For a new candidate concept, the system queries an LLM to determine whether a relationship exists between the new concept and *every single existing node* in the graph, one at a time. No narrowing or filtering occurs before this stage. This mirrors the approach explicitly identified as impractical at scale in \[1\], and its cost is expected to grow directly, without bound, as the graph grows — providing the reference curve against which the other three strategies are compared.

### 3.2.3 Strategy B: Embedding-Similarity Thresholding

This strategy first computes a vector embedding for the new candidate concept, then computes cosine similarity between this embedding and the pre-computed embeddings of every existing node in the graph. Only nodes whose similarity exceeds a fixed threshold are shortlisted for the final LLM decision step. Following the precedent established in \[2\], an initial threshold value of approximately 0.6 is adopted as a starting point, subject to empirical calibration on this study's own dataset as described in Section 3.4.

### 3.2.4 Strategy C: Approximate Nearest-Neighbor (ANN) Retrieval

This strategy also relies on vector embeddings, but rather than filtering by a fixed similarity *value* (as in Strategy B), it retrieves a fixed *number* of the most similar existing nodes using an approximate nearest-neighbor index. This study implements this strategy using FAISS, a widely used open-source library for efficient similarity search, configured to retrieve the top-*k* most similar existing nodes (with *k* to be fixed empirically per Section 3.4) for shortlisting before the final LLM decision step. This strategy is structurally distinct from Strategy B specifically because it does not require selecting a single similarity threshold as its sole tunable parameter, directly addressing the open concern raised in \[2\] regarding threshold-based hyperparameter selection.

### 3.2.5 Strategy D: Bounded-Bucket Partitioning

This strategy organizes existing nodes into size-bounded groups ("buckets"), such that inserting a new node only ever requires comparing it against the members of one bounded bucket, rather than the whole graph. This study's implementation adapts the general bucket-partitioning principle described in \[4\] — specifically, the idea that bucket size is capped by a small constant, and that a bucket exceeding this cap is split while a bucket falling below a lower bound is merged with an adjacent bucket — to the concept-node insertion task, rather than reproducing EraRAG's own text-chunk-summarization architecture directly. This distinction is stated explicitly here because, as discussed in Chapter 2 Section 2.2.4, EraRAG's insertion operation (summarizing a bucket of text chunks) and this study's insertion operation (semantically placing a concept node) are not the same underlying task, even though both share the same bounded-partition *structural principle*.

### 3.2.6 The Shared Decision Step

Across all four strategies, once a shortlist of candidate existing nodes has been produced, a single shared mechanism determines the final relationship: an LLM is prompted with the new concept and each shortlisted candidate, and asked to determine whether a relationship exists and, if so, its type. This shared step is held constant across all four strategies specifically so that any measured difference in cost or accuracy can be attributed to the *narrowing mechanism* rather than to differences in how the final decision is made — this is the controlled variable that makes the four-strategy comparison methodologically valid.

## 3.3 Data Sources (SWAP w/ PREVIOUS)

### 3.3.1 Gold-Standard Data (Accuracy Measurement)

As established in Chapter 1's Scope and Limitations, this study uses two publicly released gold-standard educational knowledge graphs from \[6\], \[7\]: the Metacademy graph (141 expert-verified concepts) and the DSA graph (29 expert-verified concepts, spanning core Data Structures and Algorithms topics such as linked lists, trees, graphs, and standard traversal and sorting algorithms).

Direct inspection of the gold-standard dataset files confirms their structure: the DSA dataset file consists of semicolon-delimited triples (`source_concept;target_concept;label`), while the Metacademy dataset file consists of space-delimited triples (`source_concept target_concept label`). In both gold-standard files, `label` is `1` if the target concept is a prerequisite of the source concept (that is, `source_concept` requires `target_concept`), and `0` otherwise. In contrast, the repository's ten intermediate example MEKG files adhere to the reverse convention, where `concept1;concept2;1` indicates that `concept1` is a prerequisite of `concept2`. Both gold-standard files are structured as exhaustively enumerated pairwise judgment sets — recording an explicit prerequisite/non-prerequisite judgment for nearly every ordered pair of concepts in the graph, rather than listing only the positive edges as a sparse edge list would. This format directly supports this study's evaluation procedure: for any withheld concept, the complete, expert-verified set of true and false prerequisite relationships to every other concept in the graph can be retrieved directly from the file, without requiring any additional inference about missing or implicit relationships.

The evaluation procedure for this data source proceeds as follows. For each experimental trial, one concept is withheld from the gold-standard graph, and the remaining concepts — together with their known pairwise relationships — form the "existing" graph into which the withheld concept is re-inserted using each of the four narrowing strategies in turn. Each strategy's resulting placement (the set of relationships it establishes between the withheld concept and the existing graph) is then compared against the complete, expert-verified row of true labels for that concept, allowing precision and recall to be computed directly: a true positive is a relationship the strategy correctly identifies as a prerequisite; a false positive is a relationship the strategy incorrectly identifies as a prerequisite; a false negative is a true prerequisite relationship the strategy fails to identify. This withhold-and-reinsert procedure is repeated across multiple withheld concepts to obtain a stable average accuracy measurement per strategy, per graph size.

### 3.3.2 Synthetic/Unlabeled Data (Cost-Scaling Measurement)

To extend the cost-scaling measurements beyond the ceiling of the largest available gold-standard graph, this study draws on two complementary sources, neither of which requires expert-verified ground truth, since — as established in Chapter 1 — cost metrics (wall-clock time, LLM API call count) can be measured objectively regardless of whether the "correct" graph structure is known.

First, at the smaller-to-mid range of the size sweep, this study reuses the smaller example concept maps released in the same dataset repository as the primary gold-standard graphs \[7\] — specifically the ten intermediate-sized example MEKG files spanning 6, 7, 8, 10, 18, 21, and 35 concepts (`MEKG_with_6_nodes(DS).txt`, `MEKG_with7nodes.txt`, `MEKG_with_8_nodes(DS3).txt`, `MEKG_with_8_nodes(DS4).txt`, `MEKG_with_8_nodes(logic).txt`, `MEKG_with_10_nodes(DS1).txt`, `MEKG_with_10_nodes(DS2).txt`, `MEKG_with_18_nodes(ML).txt`, `MEKG_with_21_nodes(ML).txt`, and `MEKG_with_35_nodes(ML1).txt`), drawn from the Machine Learning, Data Structures, and Logic domains. Reusing these graphs, rather than generating entirely synthetic data at these sizes, allows this portion of the size sweep to rely on real concept data from the same trusted source as this study's primary evaluation set.

Second, to reach the larger graph sizes required by the full size sweep established in Chapter 1 (up to approximately 2,000 nodes) — beyond what any released real dataset in \[7\] provides — additional concept sets are generated using an LLM prompted to produce a large list of plausible, distinct concept names within a defined subject domain (consistent with the general domain of the DSA graph, i.e., computer science and related technical fields, to maintain reasonable topical continuity with this study's other data sources). It is explicitly acknowledged that these larger synthetic sets are not independently verified for internal correctness or pedagogical validity, and are therefore used exclusively for the cost-scaling dimension of the experiment (Research Question 1); they are not used for the accuracy dimension (Research Question 2), which relies solely on the expert-verified gold-standard data described in Section 3.3.1.

### 3.4 Experimental Procedure

For each of the four candidate-narrowing strategies described in Section 3.2, and for each graph size in the log-spaced progression established in Chapter 1 (n \= 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, supplemented at the smaller end by the DSA graph's 29-node scale and Metacademy's 141-node scale where gold-standard evaluation is required), the following procedure is executed:

1. **Graph preparation.** An existing graph of the target size is assembled — for the gold-standard accuracy trials, by withholding one or more concepts from the Metacademy or DSA graph as described in Section 3.3.1; for the cost-scaling trials, from the synthetic/unlabeled data source described in Section 3.3.2.  
2. **Insertion.** One new candidate concept is inserted into the existing graph using the strategy under test, following that strategy's specific procedure as detailed in Sections 3.2.2 through 3.2.5, culminating in the shared LLM decision step described in Section 3.2.6.  
3. **Measurement.** For this single insertion, the following are recorded: wall-clock time elapsed from the start of the narrowing step to the completion of the final decision step; the total number of LLM API calls made during the insertion; and, where gold-standard data is available, the precision and recall of the resulting placement as defined in Section 3.3.1.  
4. **Repetition.** Steps 1–3 are repeated across multiple independently withheld or generated concepts at each graph size, to obtain a stable average measurement per strategy per size, rather than relying on the outcome of a single insertion.

This procedure is executed independently for each of the four strategies at each graph size, yielding, for every (strategy, graph size) pair, an average wall-clock time, an average LLM call count, and — where applicable — an average precision and recall. These results form the basis of the cost-scaling and accuracy-scaling curves that directly answer this study's four research questions as stated in Chapter 1, Section 1.2.

### 3.5 Data Analysis

The primary analytical method of this study is the visual and statistical comparison of scaling curves: for each strategy, wall-clock time and LLM call count are plotted against graph size, allowing direct visual inspection of whether cost grows linearly, sub-linearly, or remains effectively flat as the graph scales — the specific empirical question motivating this study, as established in Chapter 1's Statement of the Problem. Where a strategy's underlying literature makes an explicit theoretical claim about its scaling behavior (most notably, the amortized bound proposed in \[4\] for bucket-based partitioning), the observed empirical curve is directly compared against that claimed bound to assess consistency, addressing Research Question 4\.

Placement accuracy (precision and recall, as defined in Section 3.3.1) is analyzed similarly, plotted against graph size for each strategy, to determine whether accuracy degrades, remains stable, or improves as the graph grows — directly addressing Research Question 2\. Finally, the four strategies' cost and accuracy curves are compared side-by-side at each common graph size, directly addressing Research Question 3 and this study's General Objective as stated in Chapter 1\.
